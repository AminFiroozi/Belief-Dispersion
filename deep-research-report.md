# Sentiment Distribution in Financial News and the Case for Going Beyond Mean Sentiment

## Bottom line

The literature gives you a strong theoretical and empirical basis for the claim that **the distribution of sentiment in financial news can contain economically relevant information that average sentiment discards**. In classic disagreement models, a mean-preserving spread in beliefs can change prices, trading volume, volatility, crash risk, and option demand even when the average belief is unchanged. In text-based finance, most canonical papers still focus on **first-moment tone** or net pessimism, but adjacent literatures on investor disagreement, media polarization, attention, information diffusion, and option-implied event risk all point in the same direction: **a neutral average can mask either genuine consensus neutrality or intense bipolarity**, and those two states should not be expected to price the same. citeturn38search0turn34search1turn31search1turn7search1turn23search0

That conclusion is strongest for outcomes that are naturally sensitive to **dispersion rather than direction**: trading volume, realized volatility, VIX-like measures, bid-ask spreads, option straddle demand, implied volatility, jump risk, and crash risk. The literature is more mixed on **expected returns**, because disagreement can push prices up under short-sale constraints and then predict lower returns later, but can also interact with uncertainty, learning, and attention in ways that attenuate directional return effects. The cleanest contribution of your project is therefore not “sentiment matters” but rather **“conditional on mean sentiment, the cross-article distribution of sentiment contains incremental information about market states and market reactions.”** citeturn38search2turn38search5turn39search2turn22search0turn35search1turn22search19

## Theoretical foundations for why dispersion should matter

The core theory begins with **Miller (1977)**. Under short-sale constraints, prices are tilted toward the views of the most optimistic investors, so greater divergence of opinion can raise prices above consensus fundamentals and predict lower subsequent returns when beliefs converge or bad news arrives. This is the classic reason disagreement matters for valuation even when the average opinion does not move much. citeturn38search0turn38search12

The older heterogeneous-beliefs literature broadens that insight. **Varian (1985)** shows that divergence of opinion can affect equilibrium asset prices in complete markets. **Harrison and Kreps (1978)** show that when investors disagree and can resell to future optimists, speculative motives can support prices above buy-and-hold valuations. **Morris (1996)** and **Banerjee and Kremer (2010)** show how disagreement about the interpretation of public information can generate speculative trade and dynamic patterns in volume and volatility. **Harris and Raviv (1993)** make the differential-interpretation mechanism especially clear: traders can share the same public signal but disagree about what it means, producing trading volume and joint price-volume dynamics. citeturn34search1turn33search3turn32search8turn32search2turn31search1

That logic maps directly onto your paper’s motivating example. A day with uniformly neutral news means most investors are receiving little directional impetus. A day with half strongly positive and half strongly negative stories is qualitatively different: the public information set is **contested**, so marginal valuations separate, trade motives intensify, and the market may need more time, more volume, and more price discovery to aggregate information. In theory, the second case is a **mean-preserving spread in narrative interpretation**, not a neutral-information state. That should raise trading intensity and often realized volatility even if the average sentiment score is exactly zero. citeturn31search1turn32search2turn31search2

Short-sale constraints make the distinction sharper. **Chen, Hong, and Stein (2002)** show that when breadth of ownership narrows—a proxy for sidelined pessimists—future returns are lower, consistent with disagreement plus short-sale constraints. **Hong and Stein (2003)** argue that hidden bearish information can accumulate when pessimists are constrained, only to be revealed during market declines, generating crash risk. **Atmaz and Basak (2018)** and **Chang, Hsiao, Ljungqvist, and Tseng (2022)** update this tradition by showing that disagreement models jointly imply overvaluation and crash risk, especially when disagreement interacts with limits to arbitrage. citeturn38search5turn31search5turn11search0turn39search2

Options provide an additional theoretical channel. **Buraschi and Jiltsov (2006)** show that in heterogeneous-beliefs economies, disagreement and model uncertainty create demand for options and affect both option prices and option volumes. **Kelly, Pástor, and Veronesi (2016)** show that uncertainty about political events is priced in equity options. More recently, **Salome, Tauchen, and Li (2024)** provide evidence of disagreement in the S&P 500 options market, while **Alexiou, Dimitriadis, and Prokopczuk (2025)** show that concave implied-volatility curves before earnings announcements often reflect a **bimodal risk-neutral distribution** and higher subsequent realized volatility. These papers are highly relevant because a bipolar news distribution is conceptually closer to an event-risk or mixture-distribution state than to a neutral state. citeturn7search1turn35search1turn29search1turn22search19

A separate but complementary channel comes from **attention and information diffusion**. **Andrei and Hasler (2015)** model how attention and learning uncertainty jointly affect volatility and risk premia. **Da, Engelberg, and Gao (2015)** show that search-based fear predicts financial market outcomes, and **Fisher, Martineau, and Sheng (2022)** show that macroeconomic attention rises around scheduled announcements and predicts announcement risk premia and implied-volatility changes. If bipolar news is more salient than uniformly neutral news, it can draw more attention and trigger stronger market reactions without shifting the mean tone much. citeturn22search0turn23search0turn22search2

Narrative economics reinforces the point. **Shiller (2017)** argues that contagious narratives matter for economic outcomes, and recent work on **narratives about the macroeconomy** measures not simply direction but the structure of explanations people use. For your project, that means distributional sentiment can be interpreted not only as “disagreement about sign” but also as a proxy for **competing narratives** or **multiple causal models** circulating at once. A zero mean born of competing stories is not economically equivalent to a zero mean born of narrative silence or genuine consensus neutrality. citeturn4search3turn37search6turn37search0

## What the empirical literature measures when it studies disagreement

The **canonical empirical disagreement proxy** in finance is **analyst forecast dispersion**. **Diether, Malloy, and Scherbina (2002)** show that stocks with greater dispersion in analysts’ earnings forecasts earn lower future returns. **Johnson (2004)**, **Sadka and Scherbina (2007)**, and **Cen, Wei, and Yang (2017)** extend the literature by linking forecast dispersion to expected returns, mispricing, liquidity, and underreaction. These papers are important because they establish that **cross-sectional belief dispersion** has predictive content distinct from the mean forecast. citeturn38search2turn25search14turn18search2

Another canonical line uses **ownership-based proxies**. **Chen, Hong, and Stein (2002)** use breadth of ownership as a revealed-preferences measure of whether pessimists are shut out by short-sale constraints. The breadth measure is not text-based, but it is directly relevant to your paper because it shows that disagreement affects prices through the **distribution of who is willing or able to hold the asset**, not only through the average opinion in the market. citeturn38search5turn38search17

A third line uses **trading- and event-based disagreement proxies**. **Kandel and Pearson (1995)** document that investors differentially interpret the same public signal, producing trade in speculative markets. **Garfinkel and Sokobin (2006)** show that abnormal volume around earnings announcements is consistent with opinion divergence and helps explain post-earnings-announcement drift. **Antweiler and Frank (2004)** use internet message-board content to extract both bullishness and disagreement, finding that online discussion helps predict volatility and trading volume more than directional returns. citeturn38search7turn18search8turn0search7

More recent work measures disagreement directly from **social media**. **Cookson and Niessner (2020)** use StockTwits sentiment and user types to separate disagreement caused by different information sets from disagreement caused by different investment approaches. They show that disagreement is tightly linked to trading behavior. **Giannini, Irvine, and Shu (2019)** study convergence and divergence of Twitter opinions around earnings announcements and find strong volume effects. **Al-Nasseri et al. (2018)** also find that online disagreement predicts higher trading volume and that its return effects vary with market states. citeturn18search3turn18search13turn10search5turn10search4

There is also now a small but growing literature on **news disagreement and media polarization**. **Goldman, Ma, and Xie (2024)** show that conservative and liberal newspapers polarize the reporting of the same corporate financial news, and that this politics-induced disagreement raises abnormal trading volume for politically extreme firms. **Li and Zong (2025)** construct a **news-based investor disagreement** measure around firm news and find negative cross-sectional return predictability, interpreting disagreement through the volume-volatility elasticity around news. These papers are especially close to your question because they use disagreement embedded in **news content or reactions to news**, not just in forecasts or holdings. citeturn16search1turn16search4turn29search10

Outside equities, disagreement has also been measured in **options**, **currencies**, **household surveys**, and **mutual fund portfolios**. **Buraschi and Jiltsov (2006)** and **Salome, Tauchen, and Li (2024)** use option-market quantities and prices; **Beber, Buraschi, and Breedon (2010)** use foreign-exchange forecast dispersion; **Li and Li (2021)** show that household belief dispersion predicts aggregate stock trading volume; and **Jiang and Sun (2014)** use dispersion in beliefs among active mutual funds. Collectively, these papers make the empirical point you need: disagreement is not confined to analyst forecasts and shows up across multiple data-generating processes. citeturn7search1turn29search1turn34search13turn17search3turn25search1

## Mean sentiment versus higher moments and distributional features

The **established** text-based asset-pricing literature is overwhelmingly first-moment oriented. **Tetlock (2007)** shows that pessimistic media tone helps explain market behavior; **Tetlock, Saar-Tsechansky, and Macskassy (2008)** show that word-based measures in news capture firm fundamentals; **Loughran and McDonald (2011)** and **Jegadeesh and Wu (2013)** develop better finance-specific textual measurement; and **Loughran and McDonald (2016)** survey textual analysis in accounting and finance. In other words, the field’s center of gravity has been **average tone, net pessimism, and weighted word scores**. citeturn0search5turn5search16turn14search0turn15search0turn14search8

That does **not** mean distributional features are absent; it means they are more scattered across adjacent literatures. **Antweiler and Frank (2004)** explicitly separate **bullishness** from **disagreement** in message-board data. **Tetlock (2011)** introduces **staleness**, a textual-similarity measure showing that the market reacts less to repeated stories and that stale-news price reactions partially reverse, which is not a pure first-moment sentiment result. **Bybee, Kelly, Manela, and Xiu (2020)** model the **topic structure** of economic news, showing that the distribution of topics, not only net tone, tracks macro and financial series. These papers point toward a richer distributional view of text. citeturn0search7turn21search4turn14search7

A **suggestive** but not yet fully canonical literature goes further toward your exact idea. **Goldman, Ma, and Xie (2024)** study **political polarization in financial news**. **Xue, Wang, Dong, and Cao (2026)** study **cross-channel tone volatility**, effectively treating variation in tone across news channels as an information variable. **Li and Zong (2025)** estimate **news-based disagreement**. These papers say, in different ways, that the **dispersion or polarization of text** can matter beyond the mean. citeturn16search1turn16search4turn12search0turn12search13turn29search10

Some related findings come from financial derivatives rather than the news itself. **Alexiou, Dimitriadis, and Prokopczuk (2025)** show that **concave implied-volatility curves before earnings** often imply a **bimodal risk-neutral distribution**, and those curves predict larger absolute post-announcement moves and higher realized volatility. Conceptually, that result is very close to your “bipolar mean-zero versus uniformly neutral mean-zero” distinction: two states can imply the same expected move while containing radically different higher moments and event-risk implications. citeturn22search19

My judgment is that **sentiment variance, entropy, skewness, kurtosis, tail mass, and bimodality in financial news remain open territory in mainstream finance**. There are pieces of the idea in disagreement, polarization, tone-volatility, attention, and option-implied event-risk papers, but the field does not yet appear to have a dominant canonical paper showing that the **within-period cross-article sentiment distribution** robustly forecasts markets after controlling for mean sentiment. That is the cleanest research gap your paper can fill. citeturn14search8turn16search1turn12search0turn29search10turn22search19

## Which market outcomes distributional sentiment should plausibly predict

The strongest **established prediction** is for **trading volume**. Differential-interpretation theories and empirical studies consistently tie disagreement to higher trade. Harris and Raviv’s theory, Kandel and Pearson’s evidence on public-signal interpretation, Garfinkel and Sokobin’s earnings-announcement study, Antweiler and Frank’s online discussion measures, and Cookson and Niessner’s StockTwits evidence all point in the same direction. If your dispersion measure does not predict volume, that would be surprising. citeturn31search1turn38search7turn18search8turn0search7turn18search13

The next strongest prediction is for **realized volatility and jump risk**. Disagreement widens the set of plausible valuations and increases the amount of trading required for prices to aggregate information. **Atmaz and Basak (2018)** predict that volatility rises with belief dispersion. **Andrei and Hasler (2015)** show that attention and learning uncertainty raise return variance. **Baker, Bloom, Davis, and Kost** show that newspaper-based volatility trackers contain substantial information about future market volatility, and **Manela and Moreira’s** news-implied-volatility tradition links news to disaster concerns. For firm-level studies, dispersion should therefore be especially promising as a predictor of next-day or next-week realized volatility and of the probability of large absolute moves. citeturn11search0turn22search0turn13search14turn24search9

For **option markets**, the case is extremely strong. **Buraschi and Jiltsov (2006)** connect belief differences to option prices and volumes. **Kelly, Pástor, and Veronesi (2016)** show that uncertainty is priced in options around political events. **Salome, Tauchen, and Li (2024)** show disagreement in index options. And **Alexiou et al. (2025)** show that option surfaces encode bimodal event distributions. A news-dispersion measure should therefore plausibly predict **ATM straddle demand, implied volatility changes, skew/smirk changes, option volume, and post-news absolute returns**. citeturn7search1turn35search1turn29search1turn22search19

For **expected returns**, the prediction is more conditional. Miller-style theory plus short-sale constraints says high disagreement can mean temporary overpricing and thus lower subsequent returns. That is broadly consistent with **Diether, Malloy, and Scherbina (2002)**, **Chen, Hong, and Stein (2002)**, and **Chang et al. (2022)**. But disagreement can also coexist with rational information arrival, with uncertainty and attention, or with event-risk compensation, so a simple monotone return effect is less universal than the volume or volatility effect. In practice, I would treat return predictability as an important but secondary test, not the central make-or-break test of the project. citeturn38search2turn38search5turn39search2

For **reversals and crash risk**, the literature is favorable. **Hong and Stein (2003)** imply that disagreement under short-sale constraints stores up hidden bad news that is released in downturns, while **Chang et al. (2022)** explicitly test disagreement models using firm-level text and show implications for overvaluation and crash risk. A bipolar sentiment state with mean zero should therefore be more likely than a uniformly neutral state to precede **negative skewness, drawdowns, crash-like realizations, or sharp post-announcement repricing**. citeturn31search5turn39search2

For **liquidity and bid-ask spreads**, the case is plausible but somewhat less settled. Text and sentiment can accelerate price discovery but worsen liquidity if they trigger aggressive trading or adverse-selection concerns. **Baker and Stein (2004)** tie liquidity to sentiment under short-sale constraints. **Sadka and Scherbina (2007)** show links between analyst disagreement, mispricing, and liquidity. **von Beschwitz, Keim, and Massa (2020)** show that news analytics speed price and volume reaction but reduce liquidity. That suggests a high-dispersion news state should predict **wider spreads and lower depth**, especially when disagreement coincides with attention spikes. citeturn27search0turn25search14turn20search15

## How to separate disagreement from uncertainty, attention, and asymmetric information

A central empirical challenge is that **disagreement**, **uncertainty**, **attention**, and **asymmetric information** often move together but are not the same object. The cleanest distinction is conceptual. **Disagreement** is cross-sectional dispersion in beliefs across agents; **uncertainty** is the width of the distribution each agent assigns to outcomes; **attention** is how much cognitive or media weight an issue receives; **asymmetric information** is inequality in information sets that creates adverse selection and informed-trading frictions. The macro-forecast literature is very useful here: **Lahiri and Sheng (2010)** show that disagreement is not the same as uncertainty, and **Krüger, Lerch, and Naujoks (2016)** show that the full distribution of forecasts can help separate the two. citeturn17search17turn17search11turn17search8

For your setting, I would treat **mean sentiment** and **sentiment dispersion** as separate by construction, but then include explicit non-text controls for the other nearby constructs. For **uncertainty**, the canonical controls are **EPU**, the **Jurado-Ludvigson-Ng uncertainty measure**, and text-based volatility trackers such as **EMV/NVIX**. For **attention**, the canonical controls are news counts, search intensity, and article-count-based attention indexes such as those in **Da, Engelberg, and Gao (2015)** and **Fisher, Martineau, and Sheng (2022)**. For **information diffusion**, controls or interaction terms based on coverage intensity, staleness, dissemination channel, and republishing are standard, drawing on **Tetlock (2011)**, **Peress (2014)**, and **Engelberg and Parsons (2011)**. citeturn8search22turn4search2turn13search14turn24search9turn23search0turn22search2turn21search4turn21search1turn28search1

You should also distinguish **directional disagreement** from **non-directional uncertainty**. A useful way to do that is to decompose the text distribution into at least two orthogonalized pieces: one capturing **mean tone**, one capturing **dispersion/polarization**, and another capturing **overall ambiguity or uncertainty-language intensity**. If dispersion predicts volume, volatility, spreads, or straddle demand even after controlling for mean tone and uncertainty-language measures, that is much closer to a true disagreement interpretation. This decomposition is strongly motivated by the fact that uncertainty measures and disagreement measures do not behave identically in the macro literature. citeturn17search11turn17search8turn15search13

A further distinction is between disagreement and **asymmetric information**. If your dispersion signal mainly reflects informed-versus-uninformed trading, you should expect stronger effects on spreads, price impact, and short-horizon price discovery than on purely opinion-driven volume. The clearest tests are to see whether your signal continues to matter after controlling for **short interest**, **liquidity measures**, **pre-news drift**, **staleness**, and **retail attention**, and whether the effect is concentrated in settings with greater scope for informed trading. Papers such as **Cookson et al.** on disagreement facilitating informed trading and **von Beschwitz et al.** on the liquidity effects of news analytics are helpful interpretive benchmarks. citeturn19search1turn20search15

## Credible designs, controls, and identification issues

A plain daily predictive regression is a reasonable starting point, but the most credible version is a **firm-day panel** in which the key regressor is a cross-article sentiment-distribution feature observed by market close, and the incremental benchmark is **mean sentiment** over the same article set. Standard controls should include lagged returns, lagged realized volatility, volume, turnover, Amihud-type illiquidity or spreads, size, book-to-market, analyst coverage, article count, source count, topic composition, earnings-announcement indicators, market-wide volatility controls, and attention controls such as search or media counts. These controls are standard because disagreement, sentiment, attention, and liquidity are tightly entangled in the literature. citeturn18search8turn25search14turn27search0turn23search0turn21search1

For identification, the biggest threats are **simultaneity and reverse causality**. Media tone and narrative dispersion may respond to prices rather than predict them. **Engelberg and Parsons (2011)**, **Peress (2014)**, and **Dougal et al. (2012)** show why causal media identification is difficult and why exogenous variation in coverage matters. **Ahern and Sosyura (2014)** further show that firms can actively shape the news flow, which means source composition is endogenous. In your setting, the key implication is that “same-day correlation” is not enough. You need designs that tighten timing or exploit plausibly exogenous news events. citeturn28search1turn28search8turn20search0turn28search2

Event clustering and publication timing are another serious concern. Financial news and announcements bunch around earnings, macro releases, and crisis episodes; managers also strategically release bad news at low-attention times. **DellaVigna and Pollet (2009)** and **deHaan, Shevlin, and Thornock (2015)** show why timing and attention effects can distort standard event studies. That means your baseline should control for **day-of-week**, **time-of-day**, **scheduled-announcement windows**, and possibly **within-day market conditions**, and your cleanest identification should come from narrow windows around predetermined releases such as earnings, FOMC, CPI, or payrolls. citeturn13search0turn13search1

The most convincing design for your question is probably a **stacked combination** of three approaches. First, a **daily out-of-sample forecasting design** for volatility, volume, and option-implied volatility, where distributional sentiment must beat mean sentiment and article counts. Second, **intraday event studies** around scheduled macro and earnings announcements, where you measure pre-release narrative polarization and post-release price discovery, volume, spreads, and option repricing. Third, an **option-market design** testing whether higher sentiment dispersion predicts larger changes in implied volatility, stronger straddle returns, steeper skew adjustments, or larger post-event absolute returns. The announcement literature and option-event-risk literature make these designs especially credible. citeturn30search0turn22search2turn22search19turn35search1turn29search1

Robustness should be unusually demanding. Because text variables invite specification mining, you should pre-specify a small family of distributional measures—say variance, entropy, positive-negative balance, tail mass, and a bimodality coefficient—and then report **multiple-testing-aware inference** or at least a disciplined horse race. **Harvey, Liu, and Zhu (2016)** is the standard citation for tougher significance thresholds in empirical asset pricing. You should also run placebo tests based on shuffled timestamps, shuffled source identities, and randomly reweighted article subsets; stability tests across news vendors; and leave-one-source-out tests to ensure the result is not driven by one outlet or one article format. citeturn6search5turn6search11

A useful additional design is a **difference-in-differences or stacked-event framework** built around exogenous information arrivals that are common across firms or sectors, such as FOMC, CPI, payrolls, FDA decisions, major court decisions, or industry-specific regulatory announcements. If your distributional sentiment measure is formed from news released just before or just after a scheduled event, and it predicts cross-firm differences in volume, volatility, or option reactions after controlling for mean sentiment, that is far more persuasive than unconditional time-series predictability alone. The logic is similar to the identification strategies in the macro-announcement, media-causality, and options-event literatures. citeturn30search0turn28search1turn22search2turn22search19
